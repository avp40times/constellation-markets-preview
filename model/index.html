On currencies. This model runs in USD throughout; where the Constellation deck's own figures (opex, raise, licence fees) are in GBP, I've converted them at today's rate of about $1.34. The peer figures in this table are left in each broker's own reporting currency — Plus500 and XTB's CAC in USD, CMC and IG in GBP, XTB's broader results in EUR or PLN — rather than converted, so as not to misrepresent sourced data; they inform the model's defaults as ratios and trends, not as absolute dollar figures.

What this changes in the model. XTB is the clearest warning for the "buy growth" lever: as its client base grew 24-fold, revenue per active client collapsed, not just acquisition cost. There's a slider for that effect, now set to the base case assumption of 12%, well under the 76% XTB saw, because that figure also reflects six years of product mix shift (XTB added stocks, ETPs and lower-margin instruments), not acquisition speed alone — XTB remains useful as an upper bound, not as the primary source.

What this doesn't settle. CMC's 80% "client income retained" is a different metric to this model's A-book cost or B-book result sliders, and one data point from 2022 is not a trend, so I haven't used it to move either. IG's revenue-per-client figure is my own division of two reported totals, not a number IG publishes, and its one-year comparison doesn't show whether a 36-month average client life is too short or too long for a more institutional client base. Most of the funnel and book defaults in this draft are now base case assumptions rather than peer-derived, with the peer data kept as corroborating evidence, not the primary source.

Limits of this draft

  • One blended client. No client tiers, regions or IB-introduced clients as separate groups.
  • Shock sizes are my assumptions, not fitted to a real broker's history. Book data from a target would replace them.
  • The 36-month average life, the 50%-churned-within-a-year base case, the 30% A-book share, the 25% A-book cost and the 3% float yield are all base case assumptions for a brokerage like this, not drawn from a listed peer's disclosure. They reflect one practitioner's judgement, not an audited figure.
  • New clients a month are now derived from a marketing budget (a share of the previous month's revenue) and a cost-to-acquire figure, rather than typed in directly. That closed-form relationship is my own construction to match that feedback, not something any of the cited brokers disclose directly.
  • Client cash balances (the float) that interest income is earned on are an assumption, bracketed by Plus500's disclosed range, since no broker publishes this for a target like Constellation's.
  • Month-to-month shock sizes (how much trading activity, the B-book result, new clients and churn each swing from month to month) are no longer sliders at all — they're fixed at internal base-case assumptions, following feedback that the earlier granular version was more detail than this study needs. They still drive the simulated paths behind the revenue range, variability and investor-return charts; they just can't be adjusted from the rail.
  • The ARPU-decline-on-growth slider's default is the base case assumption; XTB's six-year trend, which bundles acquisition pace with product-mix change, is kept only as an upper-bound reference.
  • Exit multiple and purchase price are placeholders, because the deck gives neither.
  • The management revenue share starts at 0% because the deck gives no rate. Move the slider to see what it costs.
  • Single currency and one acquisition. The second deal the roadmap expects in year two is not modelled.
  • The roadmap's headcount path sets the pace of cost savings. The migration loss is one event in one month.

How the Monte Carlo simulation works

This is what sits behind the three charts above that link here: the revenue range, the variability breakdown, and the investor-return distribution. Every other chart on the page (the client stack, the survival curve, the EBITDA tornado, the P&L table) is the single, shock-free base case — this is the only place randomness enters the model.

The engine runs the five-year path 300 times. Each run, every month gets a random shock from two separate channels: a book and market channel (how far trading activity and the B-book result swing from their base-case levels that month) and a funnel channel (how far new client numbers and the churn rate swing). Both channels are autocorrelated rather than pure noise — a strong or weak month has momentum into the next one, at the persistence rates in the table below — so a run can go on a real hot streak or cold streak, the way an actual business does, rather than jumping around randomly month to month. Each run separately carries a chance, every month, of a gap-day tail event: a sudden market move through client stop levels, absorbed as a cash loss because of negative balance protection.

Three things are read off the resulting 300 paths. The revenue range chart takes, for every month, the 10th, 50th and 90th percentile revenue across all 300 runs — so the band is how wide revenue could plausibly be in that month, not a forecast of any single path. The variability chart runs the simulation twice more, once with only the funnel channel switched on and once with only book and market switched on, and compares how much each one alone moves annual revenue, to show which channel is driving more of the swing in a given year. The investor-return chart takes the exit MOIC from each of the 300 runs (including whichever gap-day losses that run happened to draw) and plots the distribution, which is also where the "falls below the capital floor" and "below money back" statistics come from — figures the single base-case P&L cannot show, because it has no variation to be at risk from.

The shock sizes and persistence themselves are fixed internal assumptions rather than sliders (see Limits of this draft for why), listed here for transparency:

Persistence is how much of one month's shock carries into the next (0 would be pure noise, 1 would be a permanent shift) — set higher for the funnel than for book and market, since a marketing or churn problem tends to take longer to resolve than a single volatile trading month. Gap-day risk (chance of a gap day, loss per client) is separate from this table and still has its own sliders in the Book and market group.
